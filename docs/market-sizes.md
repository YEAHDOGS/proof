# Total market sizes of the vices — research brief

Brando's standing ask this brief answers: *"total market sizes for each
vice."* The campaign compares cannabis to alcohol, tobacco, cocaine, and
opioids — this is the money table behind the comparison, so a legislator can
see exactly how much cash each substance's market moves.

## The money in one paragraph

The United States alone spends **~$150 billion a year** at retail on four
illegal drugs — cannabis, cocaine, heroin, and methamphetamine — a figure that
rivals what Americans spend on alcohol
([rand_spending_2019](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR3140.html)).
Inside that, the **US cannabis market is roughly the size of the cocaine and
methamphetamine markets *combined***, and the legal alcohol market dwarfs them
all at an analyst-estimated **$543 billion** in the US alone. The global
tobacco market approaches **$1 trillion**. These figures measure different
things and come from different years and methods — the honest reading is
order-of-magnitude, not a spreadsheet that sums cleanly.

## What the money means for the campaign

1. **Cannabis is the big one, and it is already half-legal.** The US cannabis
   market ($52B all-sources, 2016) was roughly the size of cocaine and meth
   *combined* — and the legal channel alone now does $30B a year in regulated
   retail. NORML's figure matters most politically: $20B+ in *state tax
   revenue* since 2014, money the illicit market for cocaine and heroin
   produces for cartels instead.
2. **Prohibition leaves the money on the cartel side of the ledger.** UNODC's
   2010-era valuation put global cocaine profit around $84B a year — roughly
   the GDP of a small country, flowing entirely through organized crime. Every
   year without regulation is a year that money stays untaxed.
3. **The legal vices are the giants.** Legal alcohol ($543B, US) and legal
   tobacco (~$1T, global) are an order of magnitude bigger than any illicit
   market — and both are fully regulated, taxed, age-gated, and labeled. The
   campaign's argument is exactly this: the substance that kills nobody in
   overdose is the one the law keeps in the dark, while the ones that kill
   hundreds of thousands sit on regulated shelves.
4. **The Texas number is the sharpest local one.** $5.5B in Texas hemp-derived
   retail (2025, pre-shelf-pull) is money Brando's own state is moving off the
   regulated shelf — dollars a legislator can picture in their own district.

## Reading the numbers honestly (methodology)

- **Different years, different dollars.** The RAND figures are in 2018 dollars
  for 2016; the analyst figures are nominal 2024 dollars. Do not subtract or
  add across rows.
- **Modelled vs. counted.** Legal retail figures (dispensary POS data, state
  tax receipts) are *counted*. Illicit figures (RAND's model, UNODC's market
  valuations) are *modelled* from use, price, and seizure data — RAND's own
  lead author warns the ADAM arrestee program data the model leans on has
  since been discontinued, so future estimates will be harder, not easier.
- **Analyst estimates are not official statistics.** The $543B alcohol and
  $966B tobacco numbers come from market-research vendors whose methodology is
  not public; they are the best widely quoted figures, but they are not
  government data.
- **Do not sum this table.** It is not a ledger. It is a comparison.
